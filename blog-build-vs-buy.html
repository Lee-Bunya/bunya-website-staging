Should an advice firm build or buy its software?

Renting is fast and low upfront; building costs more to start but you own it. The real difference isn't cost — it's who ends up in control.

The short answer

Buying (per-seat SaaS) is fast, low upfront and maintained for you — but you rent forever, your data lives on the vendor's platform, and the software is configured to you rather than built around your firm. Building costs more upfront and is a real project, but the system is yours, shaped to your processes and in your own environment. Buy if you're smaller and a product fits; build if the software is your operating backbone and ownership matters.

Every growing advice firm reaches a point where the software question stops being "which product?" and becomes "should we keep renting, or build something that's ours?" It's a bigger decision than it looks, because the two paths differ not just in cost but in who ends up in control.

What "buy" really means

Buying — subscribing to an off-the-shelf platform — is fast to start and low upfront. You get a mature product, maintained for you, with a support team behind it. For many firms, especially smaller ones, that's exactly right.

The trade-offs show up over time. You pay per seat, every month, forever — and you never own the thing. Your data lives on the vendor's platform. You're on their roadmap, not yours: the feature you need waits until they build it. And the system is configured to you, not built around how your firm actually works — so your processes bend to the software.

What "build" really means

Building — commissioning a system shaped to your firm — is a larger upfront commitment and a real project, not a download. The reward is ownership: the system is yours, built around your processes, and you control what it does next.

The honest risk with "build" is the horror story everyone's heard — the custom project that ran over time and budget and never worked. That risk is real, and it's why how you build matters as much as whether you do. A build de-risked properly — shaped on your real data before you commit, delivered in stages, proven in a live practice first — is a different animal from a blank-sheet software project.

The question was never "can we afford to invest." You already invest millions a year serving clients. The question is what you own at the end.

The comparison that reframes it

Firms often frame build-vs-buy as "a big one-off number versus a small monthly one." That framing favours renting — but it's incomplete. Set it against what a firm already spends: cost-to-serve in Australian advice runs into the thousands of dollars per client per year, so a practice serving a thousand clients is already spending millions annually to operate. Against that, a one-off build is a fraction — and unlike the subscription you'll pay indefinitely and never own, it becomes an asset the firm holds.

When each path is right

  • Buy if you're smaller, need something running quickly, and a configured product genuinely fits your process.
  • Build if you've outgrown off-the-shelf tools, your processes are a competitive asset worth preserving, you want your data in your own environment, or you're thinking long-term about the value of what you own — including toward a future sale.

There's no universally right answer. But the firms for whom the software is the operating backbone of the business increasingly conclude that renting the backbone from someone else — forever — is the more expensive path in the end.

Key takeaways

  • Buying is fast and low upfront but you rent forever and never own it; your data sits with the vendor.
  • Building costs more upfront but the system is yours, shaped to your firm, in your own environment.
  • The "big one-off vs small monthly" framing is incomplete — set the build against millions in annual cost-to-serve.
  • De-risk a build by proving it on your data first, staging delivery, and starting from a proven live system.
