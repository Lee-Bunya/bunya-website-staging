Reviews

How do you stop client reviews slipping through the cracks?

Reviews don't slip because people are careless — they slip because the process depends on someone remembering. The fix is a cycle that starts itself.

By [Author Name], [Title]
Published [Date] · 6 min read · Placeholder article
◉Swap: article image
The short answer

Client reviews slip because the process relies on a person remembering to start them. The reliable fix is a self-starting review cycle — one that triggers automatically ahead of each client's anniversary date, prepares the booking, pack and consent on its own, and surfaces any cycle at risk before it breaches. The adviser's role shifts from driving the review to approving it.

Ask a principal where the risk sits in their firm and they'll rarely say "the annual review." Yet it's the single highest-volume commitment most advice firms carry — hundreds or thousands of them a year, each one a promise to a client and, increasingly, an obligation to the regulator. And in most firms, the whole thing runs on someone remembering.

That's the root of the problem. Reviews don't slip because anyone is careless. They slip because remembering was ever a person's job in the first place.

Why reviews slip

Three failure points show up again and again:

The trigger depends on a human

If a review cycle starts because an adviser or admin notices a date on a spreadsheet, it will eventually be missed — when someone's on leave, when the list gets long, when a busy fortnight swallows the check. A date on a list is not a system; it's a hope.

The preparation is manual and slow

Even when a review is remembered, the prep — booking, assembling the pack, pulling the latest data, chasing consent — is enough work that it gets deferred. Deferred prep becomes a rushed meeting or a pushed-back date.

Nothing surfaces the ones at risk

In a manual model, an overdue review looks exactly like an on-track one until someone goes looking. There's no ambient signal that says "these five are about to breach."

Reviews don't slip because people are careless. They slip because remembering was ever a person's job.

What a self-starting review process looks like

The fix isn't a better reminder — it's removing the human trigger entirely. A self-starting review cycle works like this:

  • It begins on its own, a set period before each client's real anniversary date — no list, no one remembering.
  • It prepares itself — booking, prep pack and consent request staged automatically, ready for a human to review rather than build.
  • It shows its state at a glance — every cycle colour-coded, so the ones at risk surface before they breach, not after.
  • It carries the follow-up — the recap, the actions and the next cycle all handled in the same flow.

The shift is subtle but total: the adviser's job changes from driving the review to approving it. The system carries the load; the human keeps the judgement.

Why it matters more as you grow

At 200 clients, a manual review model strains but survives — a diligent admin can hold it together. At 1,000 it breaks, and at 1,600 it breaks badly, because the volume simply exceeds what memory and spreadsheets can track. Firms that want to grow without adding admin headcount in lockstep have to take the review cycle off human memory. There's no other way to scale it.

Key takeaways

  • Reviews slip because of a human trigger, not carelessness — remove the trigger.
  • A self-starting cycle begins ahead of the anniversary date, prepares itself, and flags at-risk reviews early.
  • The adviser approves rather than drives; the system carries the load, the human keeps the judgement.
  • Taking reviews off memory is what lets a firm grow without adding admin in lockstep.
