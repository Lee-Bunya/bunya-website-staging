Operations

What is a "practice operating system" for advice firms?

A CRM stores who your clients are. A practice operating system runs what your firm does for them — and the difference is starting to decide which firms can scale.

The short answer

A practice operating system is a single system that runs an advice firm's entire workflow — client reviews, the admin behind them, advice production, revenue tracking and compliance — with the CRM built in, rather than a CRM sitting alongside a stack of disconnected tools and manual processes. Firms are moving to one because growth is capped by how much work a team can carry, and a system that runs the work removes that ceiling.

Most advice firms run on a familiar setup: a CRM to store client records, a scheduling tool for reviews, a document tool for advice, spreadsheets for revenue, a note-taker for meetings, and a lot of manual effort stitching them together. Each tool does its job. The problem is the gaps between them — and the people whose day is spent bridging those gaps.

A practice operating system takes a different approach. Instead of a CRM plus a dozen other things, it's one system that runs the whole business, built around how the firm actually works. The distinction matters more than it sounds, and it's worth being precise about what changes.

A CRM stores clients. An operating system runs the work.

The clearest way to understand the difference is by what each one is responsible for. A CRM is a system of record — it knows who your clients are, their history, their documents. That's essential, but it's passive: it stores information and waits for someone to act on it.

An operating system is a system of action. It doesn't just record that a client's review is due — it books the meeting, prepares the pack, chases the consent, and routes the follow-up. It doesn't just store an advice document — it drafts one. The CRM is one layer inside it, not the whole thing.

A CRM knows a review is due. An operating system runs the review.

Why firms are moving to one now

Three pressures are pushing advice firms toward this model at the same time:

1. Growth is capped by capacity, not demand

Demand for advice has never been higher, and the number of advisers has stopped growing. That means the firms that win aren't the ones that hire fastest — they're the ones whose systems carry the work that used to need another person. A practice operating system lets a firm serve more client groups on the team it already has.

2. The cost to serve keeps rising

With cost-to-serve now running into the thousands of dollars per client per year, the manual production step in front of every review and every document is expensive. Moving that production onto a system is where the margin comes back.

3. Compliance can't run on memory

Proving that service was delivered against every fee — not just that consent was captured — is what triggers remediation when it goes wrong. A system that keeps the evidence as it goes turns an audit from a fortnight of assembling files into a one-click export.

What a practice operating system actually runs

The specifics vary, but a genuine operating system for an advice firm typically handles:

  • Client records & CRM — the foundation, built in rather than bolted on.
  • The review cycle — started automatically on real anniversary dates.
  • Workflow & jobs — tasks that create and route themselves.
  • Advice production — documents drafted from the data already held, for a human to approve.
  • Revenue & pipeline — projected versus actual, live, not reconciled by hand.
  • Compliance & audit — evidence kept continuously, assembled on demand.

Build vs buy: the ownership question

There's a second decision underneath the first. Most advice software is rented per seat — your data lives on the vendor's platform, at their price, on their roadmap. An operating system built into your own environment (for many firms, their Microsoft 365) can instead be owned: your data stays in your ecosystem, and the system is yours to keep and extend. For firms thinking long-term — or toward a future sale — owning the system rather than renting it changes the calculus.

Key takeaways

  • A practice operating system runs the whole advice business; a CRM stores clients within it.
  • Its value is in action — running reviews, admin, advice and compliance — not just record-keeping.
  • Firms adopt it because growth is capped by team capacity, not client demand.
  • The strongest versions are owned and run in the firm's own environment, not rented per seat.
