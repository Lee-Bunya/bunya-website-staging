Fee-for-service: proving it, not just consenting to it

Capturing consent isn't the same as proving the service was delivered — and it's the second that triggers remediation when it goes wrong.

The short answer

Capturing a client's consent to an ongoing fee is not the same as proving the service was delivered — and it's the second that triggers fee-for-no-service remediation. Proving it requires a live link between fee and service, evidence kept continuously as work happens, and the ability to assemble a complete audit file for any client on demand. Most firms have the first (consent) and scramble on the rest.

Fee-for-no-service was the defining failure of the Royal Commission era, and it has cost the industry billions in remediation. Most firms responded by tightening how they capture consent. That was necessary — but it solved the wrong half of the problem.

Consent is the client agreeing to pay. It says nothing about whether the service was actually delivered. And under audit, delivery is the question that matters.

Consent captured is not service proven

Here's the gap that catches good, honest firms out. Your CRM almost certainly records that a client consented to an ongoing fee. What it usually can't show — quickly, or at all — is evidence that everything the client was charged for was actually done: the review held, the advice given, the contact made, the work performed.

When the licensee or the regulator asks a firm to prove service against fee, the honest answer in most practices is "yes, but proving it means assembling a folder of emails, file notes and calendar entries by hand." That assembly is where the risk lives — and where remediation events begin.

What "proving it" actually requires

Proving service against fee, continuously, means three things most firms don't have running together:

  • A live link between fee and service — every ongoing fee tied to the service commitments it pays for, checked continuously rather than at audit.
  • Evidence kept as you go — the review, the advice, the contact, all captured to the client record as they happen, not reconstructed later.
  • On-demand assembly — the ability to produce a complete, audit-ready evidence file for any client in one action, not one fortnight.

The difference between a firm that has this and one that doesn't isn't honesty — both may be serving clients perfectly well. The difference is that one can prove it on demand and the other has to scramble.

Why this is the gap the big platforms leave open

Most advice software handles consent capture well — it's a well-understood, well-solved problem. Continuous proof of service delivery is harder, because it requires the whole workflow to feed one record: the reviews, the jobs, the communications and the fees all connected. A CRM that stores clients can't do it; a system that runs the work can. That's the distinction between capturing consent and closing the exposure.

Key takeaways

  • Consent capture and proof of service delivery are two different things — firms usually only have the first.
  • Remediation risk lives in the gap: being unable to prove, quickly, that service was delivered against every fee.
  • Proving it needs fee-to-service links, evidence kept as you go, and one-click audit assembly.
  • A CRM captures consent; a system that runs the whole workflow is what proves delivery.
